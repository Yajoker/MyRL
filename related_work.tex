\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related_work}

Mapless navigation in unknown dynamic environments requires coordinating goal pursuit, risk response, and obstacle avoidance under local observations. To contextualize our approach, this section reviews traditional planning methods, Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) based navigation, and Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning (HRL) based navigation in turn, tracing the methodological evolution from explicit modeling to closed-loop policy learning, and further to temporal abstraction with subgoal-level decision-making.

\subsection{Traditional Planning Methods}
\label{subsec:traditional_planning}

Classical navigation systems predominantly adopt a modular architecture that couples global path planning with local obstacle avoidance and trajectory generation. The global layer typically produces a reference path through graph search, sampling-based planning, or optimization-based planning. Hart et al. \cite{17} proposed the A* search algorithm, which performs heuristic path search over discrete graph structures. Kavraki et al. \cite{18} introduced the Probabilistic Roadmap Method (PRM), which samples configurations and constructs a connectivity graph to characterize reachable regions in the configuration space. LaValle \cite{19} proposed the Rapidly-exploring Random Tree (RRT) for feasible path search in high-dimensional continuous spaces, and Karaman and Frazzoli \cite{20} later extended it to RRT*, strengthening its theoretical properties regarding asymptotic optimality. The local layer converts the global path into executable trajectories and control sequences that satisfy the kinematic constraints of the robot. Fox et al. \cite{2} proposed the Dynamic Window Approach (DWA), which samples feasible control commands in the velocity space for real-time obstacle avoidance. R\"{o}smann et al. \cite{21} introduced the Timed Elastic Band (TEB) method, which jointly optimizes trajectory topology and temporal parameters to improve local trajectory feasibility under dynamic constraints. To address trajectory generation under high-dimensional and collision constraints, Schulman et al. \cite{22} proposed TrajOpt, which employs sequential convex optimization and continuous-time collision checking to efficiently refine an initial trajectory into a feasible one.

For dynamic obstacle and crowd interaction scenarios, traditional methods have further incorporated risk modeling and multi-agent avoidance mechanisms. Montero et al. \cite{23} improved safety and passing efficiency in crowded environments through dynamic vigilance zones and short-range targets, demonstrating that geometric rules can still provide useful insights for risk-aware and local goal design. Some studies have also attempted to embed learning mechanisms into the planning process. Veerapaneni et al. \cite{24} learned local heuristics to reduce node expansion, Li et al. \cite{25} proposed EARN to incorporate edge-collaborative computation into planning decisions for resource-constrained settings, and Zhao et al. \cite{26} introduced E(2)-equivariant structures into graph-based planning to improve representation sharing and generalization.

These methods offer a clear separation among mapping, planning, and control, with each functional module amenable to independent design and debugging, yet they rely heavily on environment representation and cost modeling. In scenarios involving dense dynamic interactions, perceptual uncertainty, or long-range tasks, the computational burden and local-optimality risks arising from frequent replanning and parameter sensitivity remain prominent, motivating the further adoption of policy learning methods within the navigation loop.

\subsection{DRL-based Navigation Methods}
\label{subsec:drl_navigation}

DRL-based navigation typically formulates mobile robot navigation as a sequential decision-making problem, learning a closed-loop policy that maps sensory inputs to control outputs through interaction with the environment. Compared with traditional planning methods that depend on complete maps, explicit obstacle models, and hand-crafted cost functions, DRL methods can directly consume inputs such as two-dimensional or three-dimensional Light Detection and Ranging (LiDAR) scans, depth images, visual information, relative goal poses, and action histories, and output linear velocities, angular velocities, or local motion commands, yielding stronger adaptability in unknown environments, dynamic obstacle avoidance, and crowd interaction scenarios. Zhu et al. \cite{28} surveyed recent DRL navigation methods for dynamic environments, categorizing them into value-based, policy-based, and actor-critic approaches, and noted that existing studies still face insufficient generalization, inadequate training in complex dynamic environments, difficulties in real-time deployment, and the simulation-to-reality transfer gap. In LiDAR-based mapless navigation tasks, the robot typically takes local laser scans together with the relative distance and bearing to the goal as state inputs, and completes obstacle avoidance and goal reaching through a continuous control policy. Liu et al. \cite{6} used LiDAR as the primary input to learn obstacle avoidance and goal-directed policies under mapless conditions, incorporating demonstration or constraint mechanisms to reduce exploration risk. To alleviate the decision instability caused by relying solely on instantaneous observations, Li et al. \cite{8} and Samsani et al. \cite{7} introduced memory mechanisms and GRU structures, respectively, to exploit historical interaction information, while the Transformer memory method proposed by Li et al. \cite{8} further enhanced spatiotemporal modeling in interactive visual navigation. These efforts indicate that historical information and interaction modeling play an important role in stabilizing decisions under partially observable dynamic environments.

Beyond directly outputting low-level velocity actions, some studies have sought to raise the abstraction level of policy outputs or to strengthen risk assessment capabilities. Yu et al. \cite{27} proposed PathRL, which extends the reinforcement learning output from velocity control to path generation so as to better align collision avoidance, geometric constraints, and trajectory smoothness. Sun et al. \cite{9} employed attention mechanisms and categorical value estimation to enhance the reliability of collision-related decisions. Cheng et al. \cite{10} considered safety, time efficiency, obstacle avoidance, and smoothness simultaneously through multi-objective rewards. Xue et al. \cite{11} leveraged spatiotemporal reasoning and value networks to achieve socially compatible navigation. Sun et al. \cite{15} further proposed RS-DDPG, which reduces redundant updates and improves convergence through event-triggered activation and network structure adjustment, demonstrating that state-change-driven update mechanisms can enhance the adaptability of DRL-based motion planning.

Flat single-level policies, however, still exhibit structural limitations in long-range dynamic navigation. A single policy must simultaneously handle goal pursuit, local obstacle avoidance, risk response, and action smoothing, which tends to complicate reward design, destabilize training, and induce action oscillations. When dynamic obstacle density increases or the environment topology becomes complex, single-step velocity control also struggles to sustain consistent progress over long distances. Navigation decision-making therefore calls for the introduction of temporal abstraction and task decomposition mechanisms that separate long-range intent generation from local continuous control.
